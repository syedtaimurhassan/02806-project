The Uneven Curb

How NYC parking enforcement changes depending on where you stand

NYC handed out more than 16 million parking tickets in FY2025. About half came from automated cameras — school-zone speed cameras, bus-lane systems, red-light cameras — with no officer anywhere near the car. The other half came from officers working 78 precincts across the city.

Those two halves behave very differently. And within the officer-written half, enforcement is far from even — different neighborhoods get hit by different rules, at different hours, for different amounts.

01

One city, two enforcement systems

A parking ticket looks like a simple thing — a car broke a rule, someone wrote it up. The FY2025 dataset doesn't work that way.

Once the records are cleaned and separated, NYC's parking enforcement splits into two distinct systems. One runs on automated cameras — their tickets land in an administrative bucket the dataset calls Precinct 0, a code used for any violation not issued by a real street officer. The other system belongs to 78 real precincts, where officers enforce rules like street cleaning, meters, and no-standing zones on foot.

Even within those 78 precincts, the picture isn't the same everywhere. The curb changes depending on where — and when — you're standing on it.

Before anything else, a look at what the data actually contains.

What this story covers

This is about where tickets were issued and what types they were. It doesn't claim to show who was actually breaking rules, what final payments were made, or whether enforcement was fair or unfair.

02

What the data contains

The dataset is NYC's FY2025 Parking Violations Issued file — 16,250,291 records from July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025, after basic cleaning.

Each row is an issued ticket, not a paid one. Whether a ticket got paid, reduced, or dismissed is a separate question — and one the dataset doesn't answer.

NYC uses over 90 violation codes. For this project they've been grouped into readable families: street cleaning, meters, hydrant, no-standing, registration sticker, double parking, and camera-related groups. That grouping makes the patterns across neighborhoods far easier to follow.

The first thing those patterns reveal is a category that takes up nearly half the data but doesn't behave like a real place.

03

The place that isn't a place

Precinct 0 is not a real police precinct. It's the code NYC's parking system assigns to every ticket that wasn't issued by a street officer — a catch-all label for automated camera enforcement. In the FY2025 data it holds 7,524,683 tickets, or 46.3% of all records. Nearly half the dataset sits in a single administrative label that has no address, no map boundary, and no patrol officer behind it.

The breakdown makes clear why. School-zone speed cameras alone account for 63.8% of Precinct 0 records — over 4.8 million tickets from cameras placed near schools to catch speeding drivers. Bus-lane cameras, red-light cameras, and MTA bus-mounted cameras (which photograph vehicles blocking bus stops and automatically trigger fines) cover most of the rest.

The timing confirms the two-system split. Real precinct tickets peak at 9 in the morning — when officers work alternate-side parking windows, the rules that require drivers to move their cars so street sweepers can pass. Precinct 0 peaks at 3 in the afternoon, when bus-lane and MTA bus-stop cameras are busiest during the afternoon rush.

Set Precinct 0 aside, and the real geography of enforcement becomes much clearer.

Precinct 0 is real in the data but not a real place. Separating it is the first step before any geographic analysis makes sense.

04

The ticket load isn't spread equally

The 78 real mappable precincts contain 8,724,635 tickets. They don't share the load evenly. The top 10% of precincts hold 27.2% of all real-precinct tickets. The top quarter holds nearly half — 49.9%.

The same concentration shows up at the vehicle level. Of roughly 4 million vehicle keys in the data, 56.6% got more than one ticket during the year — and that repeat group accounts for 89.2% of all tickets. The top 1% of vehicles hold 14.9% of the total. One vehicle key collected 1,088 tickets across the fiscal year. Commercial and carrier plates have the highest average per vehicle, pointing to delivery fleets as the main driver of that concentration.

More tickets doesn't mean worse drivers

Higher numbers in some precincts, or from some vehicle types, doesn't mean those drivers broke the rules more often. It means enforcement was concentrated there. The dataset records where tickets were issued — not how often rules were actually broken.

Beyond the volume question, the next layer is what kinds of rules dominate where.

Concentration of issued tickets is not the same as concentration of illegal parking. The exposure side — curb length, parking demand, traffic — isn't in this dataset.

05

Each neighborhood has its own parking problem

Ticket volume is only one part of the picture. The other is what types of violations are written where.

Street cleaning is the top violation family in 44 of the 78 precincts. No-standing or no-parking leads in 12. Meter and paid parking leads in another 12.

The specialization ratios reveal the local flavor more sharply. A score above 1 means that violation family shows up more in that precinct than the city average. Staten Island precincts 120–123 sit well above average for registration and inspection sticker violations — Precinct 123 comes in at 5.54, the highest in the dataset. Precincts 34 and 44 in Upper Manhattan stand out for double parking at around 3x the city rate, which tracks with the dense narrow blocks where delivery vehicles routinely double park.

Rule type also changes the money picture — different violations carry very different fines.

The same citywide system feels completely different depending on which rules dominate near your street.

06

Not every ticket costs the same

Counting tickets treats all of them as equal. They aren't. Estimated fine value across the 78 real precincts adds up to $612,808,346, at an average of about $70.24 per ticket.

High-volume precincts are often also high fine-value precincts — but not always. Double parking carries the highest average fine of the officer-written families at $115 per ticket, so precincts with heavy double-parking enforcement punch above their weight in fine value. A precinct full of cheaper street-cleaning tickets can rank lower than one with fewer but more expensive hydrant or no-standing violations. Precinct 33 climbs 9 rank positions when switching from ticket count to estimated fine value. Precinct 78 drops 10.

The system also shifts across the day — different rules peak at different hours.

These are estimated fine values from issued tickets — not money actually collected by the city.

07

Different rules, different hours

Street cleaning violations peak at 9 in the morning — exactly when NYC's alternate-side parking windows are active and officers are walking the curb ahead of the street sweepers.

Meter, no-standing, and double-parking violations peak around 1 in the afternoon, following midday enforcement patterns. Hydrant tickets peak at 6 in the morning, when early officer shifts begin and overnight violators who risked a fire hydrant spot get found. Registration and sticker tickets peak at 8am, as officers start rounds before traffic builds.

There's no single "parking ticket hour." Every rule family runs on its own daily schedule.

The last step is being clear about what this data can and can't show.

Issue time is when the ticket was recorded. Camera records can include processing delays between the violation and the timestamp.

08

What the data shows

This project maps an enforcement footprint — where tickets landed, what kinds they were, and when. The geography is real: some precincts see far more tickets than others, some rule families dominate specific parts of the city, and the patterns are consistent enough to be worth knowing.

The honest limit is that a ticket footprint is not an illegal-parking footprint. Both halves of that distinction matter. The data doesn't have the exposure side — how much curb exists, how much demand there is, how many violations go unticketed — so drawing fairness conclusions from it alone would be overreaching.

What this analysis covers

  • where issued tickets appear in the FY2025 records
  • how Precinct 0 and real precincts differ in rule mix and timing
  • which violation families dominate each precinct
  • how ticket timing differs by rule family
  • how estimated fine value compares to raw ticket count

What it can't answer

  • violations that happened but weren't ticketed
  • whether enforcement was fair or targeted
  • actual collected revenue
  • why specific precincts look different from others
  • risk adjusted for curb supply, population, or car ownership

The strongest reading: NYC's parking enforcement has a geography, and that geography is not uniform.

09

Methods and sources

The explainer notebook covers the full technical process: data loading and cleaning, violation-family grouping, Precinct 0 treatment, precinct filtering and GeoJSON joins, fine estimation from published violation-code tables, and hourly timing analysis.

Method in brief: keep FY2025 issued-ticket records, separate Precinct 0 from real precincts, group violation codes into readable families, map only records that join to real precinct geography, and compare patterns by precinct, rule type, fine value, and hour.
