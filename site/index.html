The Uneven Curb

How NYC's Parking-Ticket System Changes Depending on Where You Stand

NYC issued millions of parking tickets in FY2025. At first, that looks like one giant citywide machine.

But once we separate administrative records from real curbside precinct enforcement, a stranger and more interesting pattern appears: the parking-ticket system changes depending on where, when, and what kind of rule you are looking at.

This project asks what the FY2025 issued-ticket dataset can show directly: the enforcement footprint across administrative buckets, real mappable precincts, violation families, ticket timing, and estimated fine value.

01

One city, several ticket systems

A parking ticket looks simple from the outside: a car, a curb, a rule, and a piece of paper or camera record. The FY2025 dataset is not that simple.

Once the records are separated, NYC's parking-ticket system looks layered. Some records sit in an administrative bucket. Some belong to real mappable precincts. Some are mostly street cleaning, meters, hydrants, sticker checks, no-standing rules, or camera-related families.

That is the core idea of the story: the curb changes depending on where and when you meet it.

Before mapping anything, we need to know what is actually inside the ticket file.

Safe project claim

This story is about the enforcement footprint of issued tickets. It does not claim to measure every parking-rule event, final payment outcome, or why a ticket was issued.

02

What we are looking at

This project uses NYC's FY2025 Parking Violations Issued dataset. After basic cleaning, the analysis keeps 16,250,291 issued ticket records from July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025.

Each row is an issued ticket record. That matters. An issued ticket is not the same as a paid ticket, a final payment record, or a complete count of every parking-rule event in the city.

The dataset is strongest when used for what it can show directly: where issued tickets appear in the records, which violation families they belong to, when they were issued, and how estimated fine value changes the picture.

Once the file is cleaned, the first surprise is that one of the biggest "places" in the data is not really a place at all.

03

The place that is not a place

The first surprise is Precinct 0. It sounds like a normal precinct, but it should not be read like a neighborhood.

In the cleaned FY2025 data, Precinct 0 contains 7,524,683 issued tickets, or 46.3% of retained records. That is almost half the dataset sitting in a category that cannot be mapped like a regular place.

Its violation-family mix also looks different. Precinct 0 is dominated by camera and administrative-style families such as school-zone speed, bus lane / bus stop, red light, and MTA camera double parking.

With this administrative bucket separated, the real curbside geography becomes much clearer.

Precinct 0 is analytically important, but it is not normal geography. Separating it makes the real curbside geography clearer.

04

The real curb is not evenly shared

After separating Precinct 0 and unmappable precinct labels, the analysis focuses on 78 real mappable precincts. These precincts contain 8,724,635 issued tickets.

The real curbside ticket footprint is not spread evenly. The top 10% of mappable precincts account for 27.2% of real-precinct tickets. The top 25% account for 49.9%.

That does not make the map a scorecard of what drivers do. It means the issued-ticket enforcement footprint is concentrated in some precincts more than others.

But ticket volume is only the first layer; the next question is what kinds of rules dominate in different places.

This map shows where issued tickets appear in the records. It does not include the exposure data needed to compare parking demand or curb supply across precincts.

05

Different precincts, different parking rules

Ticket volume is only one layer. The next question is what kinds of parking rules show up in different places.

Among real mappable precincts, Street cleaning is the dominant family in 44 precincts. No standing / no parking leads in 12 precincts. Meter / paid parking leads in 12 precincts.

The specialization heatmap adds local flavor. A ratio above 1 means a violation family is more prominent in that precinct than it is citywide among real precincts.

Rule type also matters because not every issued ticket carries the same estimated dollar amount.

The same citywide system can feel different depending on which violation families are most visible around a local curb.

06

Tickets are counted once, but they do not cost the same

A ticket count treats every ticket as one unit. But not every ticket has the same dollar value.

Using the notebook's fine-estimation logic, real mappable precincts add up to $612,808,346.26 in estimated fine value, with an average estimated fine of about $70.24 per ticket.

High-ticket precincts are often also high estimated-fine-value precincts. But the rankings are not identical because rule mix matters.

The same system also changes across the day, so the next layer is ticket timing.

The dataset contains issued tickets, not payment outcomes, dismissals, reductions, or collected money.

07

The uneven curb has a clock

The curb also changes across the day. Different violation families peak at different hours.

Street cleaning peaks at 9:00. Meter / paid parking, no standing / no parking, and double parking peak around 13:00. Hydrant tickets peak at 6:00, while registration / inspection sticker tickets peak at 8:00.

There is no single parking-ticket time. Each rule family has its own daily rhythm.

The final step is to be explicit about what this story can show and where it stops.

Issue time is the timestamp recorded on the ticket. It may not always be the exact moment a violation began, and some administrative or camera patterns may include processing artifacts.

08

What this shows — and what it does not

This project is useful because it stays inside what the FY2025 issued-ticket dataset can actually support.

It can describe an enforcement footprint. It cannot explain every driver decision, every unticketed event, or every reason a precinct looks different from another precinct.

This project can show

  • where issued parking tickets appear in the FY2025 data
  • how records differ between Precinct 0 and real mappable precincts
  • which violation families dominate different precincts
  • how ticket timing differs by rule family
  • how estimated fine value differs from raw ticket counts

This project cannot prove

  • who parked illegally but did not receive a ticket
  • whether enforcement was fair or unfair
  • whether specific demographic groups were targeted
  • actual collected revenue
  • causal reasons behind precinct differences
  • risk per driver, resident, car, curb space, or meter

The safest reading is not "this proves unfairness." The safer and stronger reading is: the issued-ticket system has a geography, and that geography is not uniform.

For readers who want the technical trail, the final section points to the notebook, artifacts, and sources.

09

Methods, sources, and references

The website is the short version. The explainer notebook contains the technical trail: data loading, cleaning, violation-family mapping, Precinct 0 treatment, real-precinct filtering, GeoJSON joins, fine estimation, timing analysis, and diagnostic checks.

Method summary: keep FY2025 issued-ticket records, separate Precinct 0 from real mappable precincts, group violation codes into readable families, map only records that can join to precinct geography, estimate fine value from the notebook logic, and compare issue-time rhythms by family.
